Research

Ecological interactions in a changing world

Our projects ask how species interactions assemble, persist, and reorganize across disturbances and human-modified landscapes.

Core questions. How can degraded communities be restored to promote stability and evolutionary potential? How do demographic and evolutionary histories relate to network roles? When do interaction patterns create resistance, redundancy, or vulnerability? How do species' roles connect across multiple ecological network layers?

Floral enhancements in harvested forests

Can managed forests support healthy bee communities?

Intensively managed forests are economically important landscapes and can also provide pollinator habitat. We test whether native floral enhancements in harvested stands increase floral resources, bee abundance and diversity, and the persistence of pollinator populations.

This work focuses on the Pacific Northwest and connects restoration experiments with questions about stand structure, post-harvest succession, and pollinator health.

Collaborators: Lauren Grand (OSU Extension), Katie Moriarty (NCASI), Jim Rivers (OSU Forestry), and regional land-management partners.

Pollinators and fire

How does fire shape biodiversity, community resistance, and pollinator health?

Large, high-severity fires are changing the spatial structure of western forests. We investigate how burn history, severity, patch configuration, and post-fire management influence flowering plants, pollinators, species interactions, and parasites.

Current work includes landscapes affected by the 2020 fires in Oregon and northern California as well as longer-term studies of pyrodiversity.

Madrean Sky Islands

How do ecological interactions contribute to community function and resistance?

Plant–pollinator partnerships can be specialized and difficult to replace, or broad and potentially redundant. We examine how these interaction patterns influence short-term reproduction and long-term population persistence in the Madrean Sky Islands—mountain communities separated by desert lowlands.

The project combines repeated field sampling with ecological network analysis to understand how interaction structure changes across space, time, and environmental gradients.

Collaborators: Shalene Jha (UT Austin) and Terry Griswold (USDA Logan Bee Lab).

Network structure through time is one lens for distinguishing species turnover from changes in interaction structure.

California pollinator resurvey

How have wild bee communities changed over roughly half a century?

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, Andrew Moldenke sampled pollinators along transects spanning diverse California ecosystems. Those collections provide an unusual historical baseline for asking how wild bee communities, plant–pollinator interactions, and introduced honey bees have changed with climate and land use.

With Ruben Alarcón and collaborators, we are relocating original sites and resampling them with comparable methods.

Restoring pollinator health in agriculture

Can intensive agriculture support healthy wild and managed pollinators?

Pollinators in agricultural landscapes face interacting pressures from parasites, pesticides, and nutritional limitation. Our work examines how mass-flowering crops and habitat management alter exposure to these stressors and how restoration can improve pollinator health.

The lab has also studied the incentives and constraints that shape adoption of bee-friendly management practices such as cover crops, permanent habitat, and certification programs.

Agricultural diversification and management are recurring themes in the lab's work.
